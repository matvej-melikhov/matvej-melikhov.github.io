Last week we were grinding hard on Avito's case for the hackathon. We built a homework grading platform with AI features for reviewers and a convenient interface for students and instructional designers.

### 🔳 Why It Was Great

I had never taken part in hackathons before. Especially in-person ones. It's actually really interesting. Particularly when you're teamed up with strangers and you need to bond quickly and build something truly cool in a short time.

Each team consisted of two AI Engineers and one AI Product Manager. It was a chance to try your hand at a non-standard role. Which is exactly what happened to me. More on that below.

There were many teams (about 150 people in person) tackling different cases. Thanks to the offline format, we could chat with other teams, exchange experiences, showcase our progress (just not to direct competitors), and share successes and setbacks.

You could feel the seriousness of the projects. Even though there were no major prizes per se (just symbolic swag from the sponsor companies), there was a strong sense that we were doing something important and solving real business problems, rather than working on a mere pet project. It was great that we could initiate additional meetings with the Avito team to better understand the pain point we were tasked to solve.

Working was as comfortable as it gets. ITMO has lots of cool and well-equipped coworking spaces: beanbag/lounge areas, hammocks, meeting rooms, and just cozy workstations. A very pleasant environment to work in.

Every day was packed with activities: technical workshops on development and soft-skill workshops on project defense. They also left room for some networking, like Random Coffee. We also had reflection sessions where we shared our anxieties, successes, and failures in front of an audience. Surprisingly, this turned out to be important, because sometimes you need to reflect and vent, but it's not always easy to do on your own.

### 🔳 It's Hard Being a Product Manager

The hackathon probably allowed me to look at engineering from a slightly different angle.

First, an ordinary DS/MLE rarely has to think about the product. Their task is to organize data collection, select and train the model, deploy it to prod, but not ask questions like: "Why will AI automation bring business value?" or "Who is actually going to use this product?"

Second, I think there is some antipathy toward the product manager role and a misunderstanding of what a PM is even needed for. Sometimes it might seem like they're just someone who talks endlessly, sets up pointless meetings, pings for status updates, tries to cut deadlines, while often lacking the necessary technical expertise and spouting nonsense as a result.

Yet as it happened, I ended up in the PM role at the hackathon. And I realized why it matters.

- We had to thoroughly think through the product context. Understand: Who is our user? What pain point are we solving? What value do we bring? Why are we better than alternatives? What features are needed for the MVP? How will we iterate going forward? At some point, you realize you've fallen into a rabbit hole and need to settle on something. Meanwhile, you're starting to go crazy from the uncertainty and the sheer amount of information you have to keep in your head.

- In addition, tasks had to be properly decomposed, committing to deadlines and deliverables. This was crucial so that all streams could proceed in parallel and we wouldn't have to redo work later. Here, I broke down a bit; at one point, I fell into despair because I genuinely didn't know what was best. A PM has to set the direction of product development, but I constantly wanted to ask someone, "How should we do this?".

In the end, through collective effort, we overcame all the difficulties. But most importantly, I gained valuable insights, leveled up my product thinking, and gained experience, confidence, and decisiveness in leading projects. I also sharpened my presentation skills when defending our project in front of the jury – I wasn't used to preparing for a pitch in just a few hours, but it was an interesting experience as well.

### 🔳 What the Hackathon Taught Me

1) Communication is critical. Listening to other perspectives and articulating your own. In today's AI development reality, you really should transcribe all conversations and feed them to an LLM so it can immediately analyze them and propose solutions/alternatives.

2) "Better" is the enemy of "good," especially at a hackathon when you only have a few days to deliver a working prototype. You need to nail down the product side and MVP scope as fast as possible and move straight to implementing the prototype. Only then should you polish features with whatever time remains.

3) Modern models don't just write code; they can also generate top-tier designs, UIs, illustrations, logos, and slide decks. They can even create sleek screencasts showcasing the website on their own, complete with zoom-ins, clicking cursors, etc. That was a real revelation for me.

4) Having a 20x Claude Code or Codex subscription is now baseline table stakes required for building large projects. I was lucky that two of my teammates had maxed-out tier subscriptions. I wouldn't have gotten very far on my $20 Pro plan. Thankfully, it was just barely enough to refine the product, generate design code, and fix bugs.

### 🔳 The Takeaway

It was a tough week, but we finished the project. Far from everything we wanted made it into the build. But that's what a hackathon is all about: delivering a prototype that you can "sell."

Unfortunately, we didn't make the top three. We'll tell everyone we took fourth place. Most likely, technical execution let us down. We got too deep into the product side and slightly miscalculated the time needed for the tech stack.

Even so, I'm pleased. I definitely recommend everyone try this format at least once. For me, it's still not the ideal fit: I don't enjoy such rigid time constraints and the feeling of looming deadlines, but the experience was undeniably worthwhile. Once again, I'm glad I chose to study here and met so many awesome people. I think the best is yet to come!
